Neil Postman

finished August 7, 2026 · audiobook

Show notesHide

Reflections

A disturbing book. At several points he compares his present unfavorably to some point in the past when people had better habits of mind, and even though it's only 40 years later it's pretty rough to compare his present to ours. At one point he talks about how "our current president" would be unlikely to be able to deliver a particular complex argument (I think he was comparing him to Abraham Lincoln), saying he (Reagan) was a former actor and more concerned with the visuals of television etc. than rhetoric. Buddy, get a load of our current president.

I can see how it might be thought of as tiresome but I think it does a good job of not being longer than it needs to be, which is obviously rare. (Also, I mean...Mr. Postman would probably note that people used to be able to read long books.)

I thought as I listened to the book about when there is new technology and a group of people will say a version of "well people said the same thing about the invention of [a previous technology]". That is such a frustrating and tired argument, mostly because it is the kind of argument that is true until it isn't any more. Things can be similar but also have differences that matter, differences that should change our understanding and calculus about the risk or benefits or dangers something offers. This feels especially important in 2026 (and maybe, by now, also obvious), but Plato being worried that printed language would cause the downfall of society might be effectively invoked when someone offers a similar argument about the printing press, but obviously people being wrong about those advances didn't mean people worried about the brain-warping power of algorithmic social media were wrong, even if at the time almost everyone brushed them off as one more Luddite.

Reflections

I was delighted by this short book. The intimacy of the scene, the humble premise both small in scale but grand in ambition: each character who walks onto the stage has only a brief moment, but you sense, even in this book, that you are being introduced to each of them to revisit them, that though they speak no or few lines while on the page, they carry a rich history worth visiting.

But this book isn't only valuable as a prequel or introduction to a series, it's such wonderful writing, and the richness of this one relationship--a mother and daughter--so vivid, so detailed and considered.
